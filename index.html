The Clark Veterans Cemetery Precedent

Lessons for the Long-Term Preservation of the Hellships Memorial at Subic Bay

Prepared for: Hellships Memorial Foundation

Purpose: Policy discussion and development of a long-term preservation agreement with the Subic Bay Metropolitan Authority (SBMA)

Date: October 2026

Executive Summary

The transfer of responsibility for Clark Veterans Cemetery to the American Battle Monuments Commission (ABMC) in December 2013 provides an important precedent for the long-term preservation of the Hellships Memorial at Subic Bay.

Following the closure of Clark Air Base in 1991, the cemetery remained on Philippine government property but was no longer maintained by the United States military. For more than two decades, local American veterans, principally members of Veterans of Foreign Wars (VFW) Post 2485 in Angeles City, undertook much of the responsibility for its upkeep.

A sustained campaign by the Clark Veterans Cemetery Restoration Association, supported by national veterans' organizations and members of the U.S. Congress, eventually resulted in federal legislation directing the ABMC to assume responsibility for the cemetery.

On December 16, 2013, the United States and the Philippines signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) establishing a framework under which the Philippine government retained ownership of the cemetery property while the ABMC assumed responsibility for its restoration, operation, and maintenance.

The agreement demonstrated that ownership of a historic memorial property and responsibility for its preservation can be assigned to different institutions through a formal agreement.

This principle is directly relevant to the Hellships Memorial at Subic Bay.

Dedicated on January 22, 2006, the Hellships Memorial honors Allied prisoners of war transported aboard Japanese vessels during World War II, including those who died during the voyages or when their ships were attacked and sunk.

The Memorial occupies a historically significant location at Subic Bay, closely associated with the December 1944 sinking of the Oryoku Maru and the suffering of Allied prisoners of war.

Although the circumstances of Clark Veterans Cemetery and the Hellships Memorial differ, the Clark precedent offers practical lessons concerning institutional responsibility, government cooperation, maintenance funding, legal agreements, and long-term preservation.

This report recommends that the Hellships Memorial Foundation pursue a formal preservation agreement with SBMA establishing clear responsibilities for the Memorial's protection, maintenance, restoration, public accessibility, and future development.

It also recommends exploring additional partnerships with the United States and other governments whose nationals are commemorated at the Memorial.

1. Introduction

Historical memorials serve purposes extending beyond the physical structures themselves.

They preserve the memory of individuals and events, provide places for remembrance, and help educate future generations.

Their long-term survival, however, depends on more than their historical importance.

Without clearly assigned responsibilities, adequate maintenance funding, and durable institutional arrangements, memorials can deteriorate even when their significance is widely recognized.

The experience of Clark Veterans Cemetery illustrates this problem.

Despite its long association with the American military and the presence of thousands of veterans' graves, the cemetery was left without a permanent U.S. government maintenance arrangement following the withdrawal of American forces from Clark Air Base in 1991.

Its eventual preservation required sustained advocacy, congressional legislation, and cooperation between the American and Philippine governments.

The Hellships Memorial Foundation faces a different but related challenge: ensuring that the Hellships Memorial at Subic Bay remains protected, maintained, accessible, and historically relevant for future generations.

The Clark experience provides a useful model for developing an institutional framework to achieve these objectives.

2. Historical Background: Clark Veterans Cemetery

2.1 Origins and significance

Clark Veterans Cemetery is located within the former Clark Air Base in Pampanga, Philippines.

Its history includes burials dating from the Philippine–American War and subsequent American military operations in the Philippines.

In 1948, remains from four earlier American military cemeteries were consolidated at Clark.

Today, the cemetery contains more than 8,500 burials, including American military personnel, Philippine Scouts, military dependents, and civilians.

More than 2,100 graves contain unidentified remains.

The cemetery represents more than a century of shared Philippine and American military history.

2.2 Closure of Clark Air Base

In June 1991, the eruption of Mount Pinatubo severely damaged Clark Air Base.

The eruption accelerated the withdrawal of American military forces, and the installation was formally transferred to Philippine control in November 1991.

Responsibility for maintaining the cemetery did not transfer to another U.S. government agency.

Although the Philippine government retained control of the property, the cemetery lacked the resources and institutional arrangements necessary for sustained upkeep.

Vegetation, volcanic debris, and deteriorating infrastructure threatened its condition.

2.3 The role of American veterans

Members of VFW Post 2485 in Angeles City assumed much of the practical responsibility for maintaining the cemetery.

Their work included clearing vegetation, maintaining graves, organizing volunteer activities, and supporting continued cemetery operations.

These efforts were undertaken with limited resources and depended heavily on volunteer participation.

For approximately two decades, the cemetery's preservation relied substantially on these private efforts.

The situation demonstrated both the commitment of local veterans and the limitations of volunteer-based maintenance for a historically significant site.

3. The Campaign for Federal Responsibility

3.1 Formation of the restoration campaign

The Clark Veterans Cemetery Restoration Association emerged as a principal advocate for renewed American government responsibility.

Among its leading figures were retired Navy Captain Dennis Wright and retired Rear Admiral Dan McKinnon.

The association argued that the cemetery's establishment and long maintenance by the American military created a compelling obligation for the United States to ensure its continued care.

Its campaign emphasized three central arguments:

1. The cemetery was an important part of American military history.

2. Veterans buried there deserved permanent and dignified care.

3. Volunteer organizations should not be expected to carry the financial and administrative burden indefinitely.

The association sought support from national veterans' organizations and members of Congress.

3.2 Congressional involvement

In March 2012, Representative Frank Guinta introduced H.R. 4168, the Caring for the Fallen Act.

In April 2012, Senators Kelly Ayotte and Mark Begich introduced S. 2320, the Remembering America's Forgotten Veterans Cemetery Act.

The legislation sought to establish responsibility for Clark Veterans Cemetery within the American Battle Monuments Commission.

Rear Admiral McKinnon testified before Congress in support of the effort.

The campaign succeeded in securing bipartisan support by presenting the issue as a matter of national responsibility rather than partisan politics.

3.3 Enactment of legislation

On January 10, 2013, President Barack Obama signed Public Law 112-260, the Dignified Burial and Other Veterans' Benefits Improvement Act of 2012.

Section 106 addressed Clark Veterans Cemetery.

The legislation authorized the ABMC to undertake restoration, operation, and maintenance following an agreement with the Philippine government.

It also authorized funding for restoration and related activities.

The legislation was a major achievement, but implementation still required negotiations between the two governments.

4. The December 2013 Memorandum of Understanding

On December 16, 2013, the United States and the Philippines signed an MOU concerning Clark Veterans Cemetery.

The agreement was signed by U.S. Ambassador Philip S. Goldberg and Arnel Paciano D. Casanova, President and CEO of the Bases Conversion and Development Authority.

The agreement established a cooperative arrangement between the Philippine government and the American Battle Monuments Commission.

4.1 Ownership remained with the Philippines

The Philippine government retained ownership of the cemetery property.

The agreement did not require the transfer of land to the United States.

This was a fundamental feature of the arrangement.

It demonstrated that preservation responsibilities could be formally assigned without changing property ownership.

4.2 Maintenance responsibility was assigned to the ABMC

The American Battle Monuments Commission assumed responsibility for restoration, operation, and maintenance.

The ABMC became the principal institution responsible for ensuring that the cemetery received professional care.

This replaced an arrangement heavily dependent on volunteers with an institutional framework supported by the U.S. government.

4.3 Long-term continuity

The MOU established an initial term of ten years, with automatic renewals for additional ten-year periods.

This provided a framework for continuity while allowing the parties to review or terminate their cooperation under defined procedures.

4.4 Defined institutional responsibilities

The agreement addressed matters including maintenance, access, operations, future burials, and coordination with Philippine authorities.

By documenting these responsibilities, the parties reduced uncertainty over who was authorized and expected to act.

4.5 A practical model for international cooperation

The agreement demonstrated that two governments could cooperate in preserving a historically significant site while respecting Philippine sovereignty and property rights.

This is one of the most relevant aspects of the Clark precedent for the Hellships Memorial.

5. The Hellships Memorial at Subic Bay

5.1 Historical significance

The Hellships Memorial was dedicated on January 22, 2006, at Subic Bay.

It commemorates Allied prisoners of war transported aboard Japanese vessels during World War II.

Thousands of American, British, Australian, Dutch, and other Allied prisoners suffered and died during these voyages.

Subic Bay has particular historical significance because of the Oryoku Maru tragedy of December 1944.

The Memorial provides a permanent place where descendants, veterans, researchers, government representatives, and the public can remember these events.

5.2 The Memorial's international character

Unlike Clark Veterans Cemetery, which is principally associated with American military history, the Hellships Memorial commemorates prisoners from several Allied nations.

Its international character creates opportunities for cooperation with multiple governments and veterans' organizations.

These may include representatives of the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, and the Netherlands, together with Philippine authorities.

This international dimension should be regarded as an institutional strength.

It also means that no single foreign government should automatically be expected to assume responsibility for the Memorial.

5.3 The need for a permanent preservation framework

The Hellships Memorial Foundation has played an important role in maintaining public awareness of the Memorial and the history it represents.

The Foundation's activities extend beyond commemoration to historical research, casualty identification, maritime archaeology, educational programs, and engagement with descendants.

However, the Memorial's long-term preservation requires arrangements that do not depend entirely on the continued involvement of individual volunteers or Foundation officers.

A formal agreement with SBMA would provide a foundation for institutional continuity.

Such an agreement could establish clear procedures for maintenance, repairs, improvements, ceremonies, fundraising, and public access.

7. Lessons from the Clark Precedent

Lesson One: Ownership and maintenance responsibility can be separated

The Clark agreement provides a practical example of a Philippine government entity retaining ownership while another institution assumes defined responsibilities for preservation.

For the Hellships Memorial, this suggests that SBMA could retain ownership and administrative authority while HMF undertakes specified preservation, educational, and fundraising responsibilities.

Such an arrangement would not require a transfer of property.

It would require clearly documented authority and obligations.

Lesson Two: Written agreements are essential

For many years, Clark Veterans Cemetery depended on informal arrangements and volunteer efforts.

Although these efforts preserved the cemetery, they did not provide the stability of a permanent institutional agreement.

The 2013 MOU established a more durable framework.

For the Hellships Memorial, an agreement should clearly address:

- Responsibility for routine maintenance and landscaping.

- Procedures for repairs and restoration.

- Authority to undertake improvements.

- Funding arrangements.

- Access for visitors and commemorative ceremonies.

- Historical interpretation and signage.

- Protection against unauthorized alterations or relocation.

- Procedures for resolving disagreements.

- Continuity if Foundation leadership changes.

Lesson Three: Government cooperation is more sustainable than volunteer efforts alone

The Clark experience demonstrates that volunteer organizations can preserve historic sites for extended periods.

However, reliance on volunteers alone creates vulnerabilities.

Personnel change, donations fluctuate, and maintenance needs increase as structures age.

A cooperative arrangement between SBMA and HMF could combine the strengths of both organizations.

SBMA possesses administrative authority and local infrastructure resources.

HMF provides historical expertise, international relationships, research capabilities, and access to potential donors.

Lesson Four: International recognition strengthens preservation efforts

The campaign for Clark Veterans Cemetery benefited from support by veterans' organizations and members of Congress.

The Hellships Memorial has the potential to attract support from an even broader international community.

Its history concerns the wartime experiences of prisoners from several Allied nations.

Formal recognition by foreign embassies, veterans' organizations, and historical institutions could strengthen the Memorial's standing and assist fundraising.

Such recognition would not necessarily involve financial commitments.

Nevertheless, it could provide valuable institutional support.

Lesson Five: Preservation requires predictable funding

One of the principal weaknesses in the pre-2013 Clark arrangement was dependence on donations and volunteer labor.

The Hellships Memorial should seek a funding structure capable of supporting routine maintenance and periodic restoration.

Potential sources include corporate sponsorships, individual donations, grants, and dedicated preservation funds.

A maintenance reserve or endowment could help protect the Memorial against fluctuations in annual donations.

The Clark precedent suggests that a formal Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) between SBMA and the Hellships Memorial Foundation would be an appropriate next step.

8.1 Duration of agreement

A ten-year initial agreement, renewable for successive periods, would offer reasonable continuity.

The agreement should include provisions for periodic review.

8.2 Protection of the Memorial

The agreement should establish that any relocation, removal, or substantial alteration requires appropriate consultation and written approval.

Where legally possible, it should also require advance consideration of the Memorial's historical and commemorative significance before decisions affecting the site.

8.3 Financial responsibilities

The agreement should distinguish between routine maintenance, major repairs, and new development projects.

This would reduce the risk of disagreements over financial obligations.

8.4 Joint coordination mechanism

SBMA and HMF should consider establishing a small joint coordination committee.

The committee could meet annually or semiannually to review maintenance, upcoming ceremonies, improvements, and preservation priorities.

8.5 Continuity of operations

The agreement should contain provisions addressing changes in Foundation leadership, organizational restructuring, or the Foundation's inability to continue particular responsibilities.

The overriding objective should be uninterrupted protection of the Memorial.

9. Potential Role of the United States Government

The Clark precedent raises the question of whether the United States might eventually provide assistance to the Hellships Memorial.

This possibility deserves consideration, but the legal circumstances are different.

Clark Veterans Cemetery was a former American military cemetery containing thousands of graves.

The Hellships Memorial is a commemorative monument established through a private initiative and dedicated to Allied prisoners of several nationalities.

There is no established basis for assuming that the ABMC could or would accept responsibility for the Hellships Memorial.

Nevertheless, several forms of cooperation may be worth exploring.

9.1 U.S. Embassy support

The U.S. Embassy in Manila could be approached regarding participation in commemorative activities, historical recognition, and appropriate preservation initiatives.

9.2 Veterans' organizations

American veterans' organizations could provide endorsements, publicity, and assistance with fundraising.

The Clark experience demonstrates the potential influence of coordinated veterans' advocacy.

9.3 Congressional recognition

Members of Congress with interests in veterans' affairs, military history, and U.S.–Philippine relations could be approached regarding recognition of the Memorial's historical significance.

Any request for federal funding or formal government responsibility would require separate legal and policy analysis.

9.4 Historical and educational cooperation

American military history institutions, museums, and research organizations may be potential partners in educational programs and historical documentation.

Such cooperation could strengthen the Memorial's public significance without requiring a transfer of responsibility.

10. A Sustainable Funding Model

A formal agreement with SBMA should be supported by a realistic financial plan.

The Hellships Memorial Foundation has already begun developing a corporate sponsorship program.

That program could provide an important source of long-term support.

A dedicated Hellships Memorial Preservation Fund is recommended.

The fund could receive donations specifically designated for maintenance, restoration, and improvements.

Potential corporate sponsorship opportunities could include annual grounds maintenance, granite conservation, interpretive signage, accessibility improvements, and support for remembrance ceremonies.

The Foundation could also seek multi-year commitments from sponsors.

Multi-year funding would be particularly valuable because it would permit predictable planning rather than relying entirely on annual fundraising.

An annual financial report could document preservation expenditures and demonstrate accountability to donors.

11. Recommended Action Plan

Phase One: Establish the institutional framework

Target: Within three months

The Foundation should request a formal meeting with SBMA leadership and its legal and facilities representatives.

The purpose would be to establish agreement in principle on a long-term preservation arrangement.

Before drafting the agreement, the parties should confirm the Memorial's existing legal and property status, including any previous resolutions, agreements, or maintenance commitments.

HMF should then prepare a draft MOA incorporating the preservation principles identified in this report.

Phase Two: Document preservation requirements

Target: Three to six months

HMF and SBMA should undertake a joint condition assessment of the Memorial.

The assessment should examine the granite panels, inscriptions, structural components, landscaping, drainage, lighting, access, and surrounding infrastructure.

It should identify routine maintenance requirements and anticipated major repairs.

The findings should form the basis of a five-year maintenance plan.

Phase Three: Establish financial sustainability

Target: Six to nine months

The Foundation should establish a dedicated preservation fund and seek corporate sponsors willing to support recurring maintenance expenses.

An annual maintenance budget should be developed jointly with SBMA.

The budget should distinguish between services provided directly by SBMA and expenses financed through HMF.

Phase Four: Strengthen international recognition

Target: Within twelve months

The Foundation should develop a coordinated outreach program involving relevant foreign embassies, veterans' organizations, and historical institutions.

The objective would be to increase awareness of the Memorial and encourage participation in its preservation.

The annual December 15 remembrance of the Oryoku Maru tragedy could serve as a focal point for this outreach.

Phase Five: Review and institutionalize

Target: Annually

SBMA and HMF should conduct an annual review of the Memorial's condition, preservation needs, funding, and public activities.

A short annual preservation report could be made available to sponsors, participating institutions, and the public.

This would help ensure transparency and continuity.

12. Conclusion

The preservation of Clark Veterans Cemetery provides an important example of what sustained advocacy and institutional cooperation can accomplish.

Following the American withdrawal from Clark Air Base in 1991, the cemetery depended for more than two decades on the dedication of local veterans.

Their efforts ultimately led to congressional legislation and a bilateral agreement establishing permanent U.S. government responsibility for the cemetery's care.

The central lesson is not that every overseas American memorial should become the responsibility of the United States government.

Rather, it is that historically significant memorial sites require clearly defined institutional responsibilities, dependable maintenance arrangements, and formal agreements capable of surviving changes in leadership and funding.

The Hellships Memorial at Subic Bay is an important site of international remembrance.

Its preservation should not depend solely on the continuing efforts of individual volunteers or the availability of occasional donations.

The Hellships Memorial Foundation and SBMA have an opportunity to establish a cooperative arrangement that protects the Memorial while respecting the authority of the Philippine government and recognizing the international significance of the prisoners commemorated there.

The Clark Veterans Cemetery precedent demonstrates that such arrangements are achievable.

A formal preservation agreement, supported by a practical maintenance plan and sustainable funding, would help ensure that the Hellships Memorial remains a place of remembrance, historical education, and international friendship for generations to come.

Final Recommendation to the Hellships Memorial Foundation

The Foundation should use the Clark Veterans Cemetery precedent primarily as a model for negotiating a durable preservation agreement with SBMA, rather than as an argument that the U.S. government must assume responsibility for the Memorial.

The immediate priority should be securing an agreement that clearly establishes:

SBMA's continuing authority over the Memorial property, the respective preservation and maintenance responsibilities of SBMA and HMF, and a cooperative mechanism for financing and protecting the Memorial over the long term.

Once that framework is established, the Foundation would be in a stronger position to seek international institutional recognition, corporate sponsorship, and potential government assistance.

The Clark agreement and legislation should be checked against the executed documents, and any proposed SBMA–HMF agreement should be reviewed by Philippine legal counsel.
